\section{Additional Experimental Evidence}\label{sec:supp-cgo}

The experiments section of the main paper retains only the figures and tables
that most directly support the central argument. This section supplies
finer-grained evidence, organized by the role each piece of evidence plays in
the argument: same-engine reorder comparisons and sensitivity analyses isolate
the ``schedule order'' degree of freedom; synthetic distributions delineate the
applicability domain of the method; a CP-SAT oracle validates the theoretical
lower bound and fixed-order optimality; surrogate-objective, working-set, and
prefetch-window experiments explain the algorithm design; and the clean/dirty
ablation isolates the $2\times$ cost gradient.

\subsection{The Role of Schedule Order}\label{sec:supp-e12}

The controlled comparison in \S\ref{sec:exp-mechanism} of the main text shows
that victim-rule differences amount to at most $4\%$ under a fixed order,
whereas the extra traffic can swing by an order of magnitude across different
orders. This section quantifies that finding through three complementary
experiments: a same-engine reorder comparison, a victim-rule sensitivity
analysis, and a visualization of the cross-order swing.

Table~\ref{tab:supp-e12} gives the controlled P2 comparison on six public
instances. All methods share the same address assignment and spill engine and
differ only in the input topological order. The differences in extra traffic
can therefore be attributed to schedule order reshaping the residency
composition inside high-pressure windows, rather than to a different allocator
or a different victim policy.

The clean/dirty-blind memory-pressure schedulers (uniform pressure and
Goodman--Hsu) are substantially worse than our order on every real case, paying
roughly $2.4$--$26\times$ more extra traffic. The critical-path-oriented CP-list
order is further behind still. By contrast, CP-free is a strong companion: by
prioritizing node releases it indirectly shortens live ranges, approaching our
order on most combinations, yet it remains clearly behind on instances such as
Conv\_Case0 where clean reserve is decisive for cost. This result shows that
reducing live bytes or releasing early is not by itself sufficient to explain
the full benefit; the difference in clean/dirty composition inside
high-pressure windows is a necessary factor.

To probe the marginal effect of the victim rule directly,
Figure~\ref{fig:supp-e2} replaces four Belady-style victim rules under a fixed
schedule order and compares the resulting change in extra traffic per case. The
four rules coincide exactly on most Matmul and FlashAttention instances; the
Conv rows show visible differences, but their magnitude remains far below the
cross-order swing. This agrees with the theoretical prediction of
Proposition~\ref{prop:belady-margin}: under distance-dominant conditions, the
marginal benefit of the victim rule is limited.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{\ksFigure{e2_victim_sensitivity.png}}
\caption{Victim-rule sensitivity under a fixed schedule order. Most Matmul and
FlashAttention rows coincide exactly; the Conv rows show visible differences,
but their magnitude remains smaller than the cross-order swing.}
\label{fig:supp-e2}
\end{figure}

Conversely, Figure~\ref{fig:supp-e3} shows the swing in extra traffic across
different legal topological orders under the same engine. Replacing the legal
topological order substantially changes the composition of evictable buffers in
high-pressure windows, and therefore produces a far larger gap in extra traffic
than fine-tuning the victim rule. Together, these two experiments confirm from
both sides that the victim rule is only a local patch, while schedule order is
the principal degree of freedom driving spill cost.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.66\textwidth]{\ksFigure{e3_order_sensitivity.png}}
\caption{Schedule-order sensitivity. Compared with fine-tuning the victim rule
on a fixed order, replacing the legal topological order substantially changes
the composition of evictable buffers in high-pressure windows, and therefore
produces a larger swing in extra traffic.}
\label{fig:supp-e3}
\end{figure}

Taken together, the three pieces of evidence support the claim that schedule
order is the principal optimization lever.
Table~\ref{tab:supp-e12} quantifies the same-engine reorder gap,
Figure~\ref{fig:supp-e2} shows that the marginal effect of the victim rule is
limited under a fixed order, and Figure~\ref{fig:supp-e3} gives the
order-of-magnitude difference of the cross-order swing. The gap in spill cost
thus comes mainly from how schedule order shapes the clean/dirty residency
composition in peak windows, and not from anything a victim rule can make up for
on its own.

This paper uses two comparison protocols. Table~\ref{tab:supp-e12} is the
same-engine reorder comparison, used to isolate the scientific mechanism;
Table~\ref{tab:supp-headline} is the end-to-end reference comparison, comparing
the complete output of each system. The former supports the claim that schedule
order shapes residency composition, the latter reflects the submission quality
of the final solver on public instances, and the two should not be conflated.

\subsection{Synthetic Distributions and the Applicability Domain}\label{sec:supp-e13}

Figure~\ref{fig:e15-applicability} in the main text shows the systematic
advantage in the capacity-bound region. This section gives the complete
per-instance data and regime statistics over all 36 synthetic instances.

Figure~\ref{fig:supp-e13} and Table~\ref{tab:supp-e13} report the distributional
results over the 36 synthetic instances. We partition the instances into
capacity-bound and order-reachable classes: the former require spilling
unavoidably under capacity pressure, while the latter leave room to avoid spills
directly through a good order. This partition corresponds to how the
spill-inevitability certificate is used in the main text: it is not a complete
feasibility test, but a way to identify the region in which the composition of
unavoidable spills must be optimized.

The results match the theoretical positioning. In the capacity-bound subgroup,
our method wins against both CP-list and random-best on every instance, with a
median $2\times$ advantage over CP-free. In the order-reachable subgroup, a good
order can eliminate spills directly, so clean/dirty shaping naturally no longer
offers a systematic advantage; here the win rate over CP-free drops to $0\%$.
The goal of the method is therefore not to dominate all DAGs, but to lower the
real cost per unit of spill when spills are unavoidable.

\subsection{CP-SAT Oracle}\label{sec:supp-e14}

\S\ref{sec:exp-mechanism} of the main text reports the summary conclusions from
the CP-SAT oracle. This section gives the per-instance data, including the
capacity-constraint ratio, the theoretical lower bound, and the fixed-order
optimal extra traffic. The small-graph oracle answers two questions: given the
order our method selects, does the address/spill engine reach the capacity-only
optimum; and do the theoretical lower bound and the area surrogate remain
consistent on exact solutions.

Table~\ref{tab:supp-e14} reports results for 8 capacity-bound small graphs.
$\mathrm{ratio}_{W^\star}=W^\star/\Capc>1$ indicates that the instance is
capacity-bound; $\mathrm{lb}_{T1}$ is the lower bound given by the
spill-inevitability certificate of the main text; and $E^\star$ is the optimal
extra traffic under a fixed order.

On all small graphs, the gap of our engine relative to the fixed-order optimum
is $1.0$, showing that for this set of oracles the bottleneck lies in order
selection rather than in the allocation/eviction implementation. The
spill-inevitability lower bound holds throughout, and
$E^\star/A\in[0.56,1.13]$, falling within the constant-factor range described in
the main text. A cross-order joint-optimum model would have to couple the
permutation variables with the residency variables, which causes CP-SAT to time
out frequently; this paper therefore makes no claim about the gap to the global
optimum, and uses the fixed-order oracle only to validate local optimality and
the theoretical bounds.

\subsection{Surrogate Objective and Diagnostic Tools}\label{sec:supp-mechanism}

\S\ref{sec:exp-mechanism} of the main text reports, in summary form, the
experimental conclusions for the overflow-area surrogate, the working-set
certificate, and the prefetch window. This section gives the per-instance data
and visualizations for these diagnostic tools, together with a quantitative
analysis of the P1 versus P2/P3 misalignment.

\paragraph{Overflow-area surrogate.}
Figure~\ref{fig:supp-e6} compares the explanatory power of the overflow-area
surrogate $\Phi$ and per-cache peak pressure for P2 extra traffic. Their
Spearman correlations are very close ($0.958$ and $0.955$). We therefore do not
present $\Phi$ as an empirically much stronger predictor; its value lies in
being cheap to compute, in being usable stably during candidate-order
filtering, and in being consistent with the area--extra approximation of the
main text.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{\ksFigure{e6_surrogate.png}}
\caption{The overflow-area surrogate $\Phi$ against peak pressure.
(a) Explanatory power of $\Phi$ for P2 extra traffic; (b) explanatory power of
per-cache peak over-capacity for P2 extra traffic. Their correlations are close;
the main role of $\Phi$ is to serve as a cheap and cross-stage-consistent
selection signal.}
\label{fig:supp-e6}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Working-set certificate.}
Figure~\ref{fig:supp-e9} reports the working-set/capacity ratio over the
near-optimal extra-traffic subset. This restriction matters: if one took the
minimum peak unconstrained across all orders, a low-peak order with very high P2
cost could wrongly weaken the capacity-bound diagnosis. For Matmul\_Case1/L1,
the near-optimal ratio is $8.5$, showing that the instance lies in a strongly
capacity-bound region where the remaining optimization room comes mainly from
the composition of spill cost, rather than from simply eliminating spills.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{\ksFigure{e9_working_set.png}}
\caption{Working-set/capacity ratio over the near-optimal extra-traffic subset.
The red entry shows that Matmul\_Case1/L1 far exceeds capacity within the
near-optimal set.}
\label{fig:supp-e9}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Objective misalignment between P1 and P2/P3.}
P1 targets lowering the per-cache peak, but \S\ref{sec:theory} of the main text
notes that a low peak is not equivalent to low spill cost.
Figure~\ref{fig:supp-e7} quantifies this misalignment: lowering the P1 objective
alone does not guarantee feasibility under capacity pressure or low spill cost.
On some instances, the P2 extra traffic of the P1-optimal order is in fact
higher than that of the order our method selects, because P1 does not
distinguish clean from dirty reserve: an order whose sole goal is a low peak may
accumulate large amounts of dirty buffers in high-pressure windows, raising the
actual eviction cost. The selection signal of the overflow-area surrogate $\Phi$
reflects multi-cache overflow pressure more directly, and so avoids this
misalignment.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{\ksFigure{e7_misalignment_worst_ratio.png}}
\caption{Misalignment between P1 and P2/P3. Lowering the P1 objective alone does
not guarantee feasibility under capacity pressure or low spill cost; the $\Phi$
selection reflects multi-cache overflow pressure more directly.}
\label{fig:supp-e7}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Prefetch-window decoupling.}
Figure~\ref{fig:supp-e8} shows the effect of the prefetch window $H$ on P3 time
and extra traffic. A nonzero window can noticeably reduce time, but extra
traffic typically stays unchanged or rises slightly. This shows that the choice
of spill set and the placement of reloads are two related but separable degrees
of freedom: P2 must control traffic conservatively, whereas P3 can trade a wider
window for pipeline overlap.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{\ksFigure{e8_decoupling.png}}
\caption{Effect of the prefetch window $H$ on time and extra traffic. (a) P3
time as a function of $H$; (b) extra traffic as a function of $H$. Prefetching
mainly moves the placement of \ksOp{spill-in}, rather than redefining the spill
set.}
\label{fig:supp-e8}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Controlled Clean/Dirty Ablation}\label{sec:supp-clean-dirty}

The ablation in \S\ref{sec:exp-mechanism} of the main text reports the $2\times$
cost difference between clean reserve, with extra traffic $1{,}536$, and dirty
reserve, with extra traffic $3{,}072$. This section gives the complete
experimental design and results, ruling out a motif artifact of the public
samples.

Figure~\ref{fig:supp-e11} uses synthetic GEMM kernels to isolate the
clean/dirty cost gradient. In (a), the DAG structure, peak, and spill count are
fixed, and only the type of reserve available for eviction inside the
high-pressure window is varied: the extra traffic of clean reserve is $1536$ and
that of dirty reserve is $3072$, exactly $2\times$. In (b), sweeping a range of
orders on the same clean instance shows that the higher the clean fraction in
the peak residency, the lower the extra traffic. This experiment rules out the
influence of the public-sample motif and directly validates the mechanism
argument of the main text concerning the $2\times$ clean/dirty cost asymmetry.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{\ksFigure{e11_synth_generality.png}}
\caption{Clean/dirty mechanism validation on synthetic kernels. (a) The
controlled ablation isolates the $2\times$ cost gradient; (b) extra traffic
decreases as the clean fraction at the peak increases.}
\label{fig:supp-e11}
\end{figure}
