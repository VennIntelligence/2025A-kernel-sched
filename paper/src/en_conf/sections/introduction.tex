NPU intra-kernel scheduling maps hundreds to tens of thousands of
micro-operations from an operator-level computation graph onto a finite on-chip
cache hierarchy. It sits at the lowest level of the compiler stacks that lower
neural operators through successive graph- and IR-level optimizations~\citep{chen2018tvm,lattner2021mlir}.
A schedule must respect data dependencies while managing the
residency and eviction of intermediate values in scratchpad-like memories. When
the live working set exceeds capacity, the compiler must temporarily spill some
buffers to off-chip memory and reload them later. Such spills add data movement
and can also block execution pipes.

Existing intra-kernel schedulers typically prioritize critical paths, resource
pressure, or peak live bytes, treating spills as a local consequence of capacity
shortage. This view misses a structural lever: once capacity
overflow is unavoidable, different legal topological orders can expose markedly
different buffer compositions inside the overflow window, and therefore incur
substantially different eviction costs.

The key distinction is between clean and dirty kernel buffers. A clean buffer is
read from off-chip memory and already has a backing copy; evicting it requires
only a later reload. A dirty buffer is produced by computation; if it will be
used again, eviction requires both a write-back and a reload. Clean and dirty
buffers consume the same on-chip capacity, yet dirty eviction costs exactly
$2\times$ as much under the traffic model. A scheduler that only lowers
the peak, or only changes victims on a fixed order, cannot fully exploit this
asymmetry. We call the scheduling freedom that controls the clean/dirty
composition of overflow windows \emph{spill-cost-aware liveness shaping}. Two
facts behind it are each known on their own: schedule order affects liveness, as
in instruction scheduling, and clean victims are cheaper to evict, as in register
allocation and cache replacement. What is new here is treating their interaction
as a degree of freedom the scheduler can actively shape in a kernel-level DAG. We
defer a detailed contrast with those lines of work to Section~\ref{sec:related}.

This paper makes three contributions:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
  \item We formulate spill-cost-aware liveness shaping for NPU intra-kernel
  DAGs. The method changes schedule order to control the clean/dirty residency
  composition in overflow windows, preserving low-cost eviction reserve when
  capacity is tight.

  \item We give a theoretical characterization of the regime in which this
  matters. The spill-inevitability certificate in
  Theorem~\ref{thm:spill-inev} provides a one-sided, linear-time diagnostic for
  identifying cases where spills cannot be avoided by any near-optimal schedule.
  The Belady-margin stability result in Proposition~\ref{prop:belady-margin}
  explains why schedule order, rather than victim-rule tuning, is the main
  optimization degree of freedom. The overflow-area theorem in
  Theorem~\ref{thm:area-extra} connects a cheap liveness surrogate to optimal
  spill traffic within constant factors under bounded absence durations,
  explaining when the surrogate is expected to track real traffic.

  \item We evaluate the method across public NPU-style benchmark instances,
  synthetic DAG distributions, small-graph oracles, and controlled ablations.
  The results show that benefits concentrate exactly in capacity-bound regions
  and that clean/dirty-blind pressure schedulers can pay
  $2.4\text{--}26\times$ more spill traffic.
\end{enumerate}
