\section{Experiments}\label{sec:experiments}

The experiments test the paper's central claim: when capacity overflow is
unavoidable, can schedule order reduce real spill cost by changing the
clean/dirty composition inside peak-pressure windows? We build evidence at four
levels: controlled reorder comparisons on public NPU-style benchmark instances,
mechanism decomposition with an applicability analysis, exact small-graph oracle
checks for the theoretical bounds, and clean/dirty cost ablations that isolate
the $2\times$ asymmetry. The four levels keep the conclusion from resting on a
single black-box comparison.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:exp-setup}

We evaluate on a set of public NPU intra-kernel scheduling instances built from
compiler-style micro-operation DAGs. They cover three mainstream operator
families---convolution, FlashAttention, and matrix multiplication---with graph
sizes from about $1.7\times10^3$ to $3.6\times10^4$ nodes, large enough to
exhibit the capacity-bound behavior typical of kernel-level scheduling
(Table~\ref{tab:benchmark}). The hardware abstraction has five on-chip caches
with abstract capacities L1 = 4{,}096, UB = 1{,}024, L0A = 256, L0B = 256, and
L0C = 512. The instances are used only for evaluation; our method hard-codes
nothing specific to any operator structure.

Table~\ref{tab:evidence} lists the four evidence layers. The common thread is a
single \emph{same-engine} protocol: every comparator and our method share one
best-fit address-assignment and spill engine (Algorithm~\ref{alg:assign-spill})

differences cleanly to how order shapes clean/dirty residency, not to
differences in the eviction implementation. We report the three views defined in
Section~\ref{sec:formulation}---P1 per-cache peak residency, P2 total spill

The main controls are drawn from the classic latency-oriented and
memory-pressure-oriented paradigms, all of which are blind to the clean/dirty
distinction. Critical-path list scheduling~\citep{kwok1999listscheduling,topcuoglu2002heft}
minimizes critical-path latency and ignores memory pressure. A uniform-pressure
greedy minimizes the stepwise peak of live bytes but treats clean and dirty
buffers identically. A Goodman--Hsu-style latency/pressure mode
switch~\citep{goodman1988integrated} alternates between critical-path priority
and pressure reduction, representing the classic integrated-prepass idea. Beyond
these, we add a \emph{free-first critical-path companion} as a stronger control:
it keeps the critical-path order but schedules ready frees early to shorten live
ranges, making the order more amenable to the allocator while still carrying no
explicit cost awareness. It tests how much of our benefit ``free early, allocate
late'' alone can explain.

Our solver keeps a practical runtime even on the largest instance
(Table~\ref{tab:runtime}). P1 emits a single schedule and finishes within
$0.2$\,s on every instance. P2 and P3 simulate the Cartesian product of
candidate orders and prefetch windows, so their cost grows with instance size,
reaching about $73$\,s for P3 on the largest instance, Conv\_Case1 (median of
three repetitions).

\subsection{Main Baseline Results}\label{sec:exp-baselines}

Figure~\ref{fig:e12} and Table~\ref{tab:main-results} report P2 spill traffic for
the six public benchmark cases under the same spill engine. The memory-aware but
clean/dirty-blind uniform-pressure and Goodman--Hsu orders are substantially
worse than our method on every case, paying $2.4\text{--}26\times$ more extra
traffic, with a median gap of about $11\times$. The pure latency-oriented
critical-path order is further behind, by roughly $8\text{--}54\times$. This
systematic gap shows that minimizing total live bytes or critical-path latency
is not enough to approach the true spill-cost lower bound: the key is to shape
the eviction reserve in peak windows toward cheaper clean bytes. Across all
$6$ cases, $3$ views, and $4$ comparators ($72$ combinations), our order attains
a lower or equal objective in every one.

The free-first critical-path companion is a strong baseline. Except
on Conv\_Case0, where it trails by $2.4\times$, its median P2 gap from our method
is only about $1\%$, and it ties on FA\_Case0 and Matmul\_Case1. This indicates
that an allocator-friendly latency order already reduces spills indirectly by
shortening live ranges, capturing part of the benefit. However, because it has
no explicit clean/dirty cost shaping, it remains systematically behind on cases
such as Conv\_Case0 where clean eviction reserve is decisive for cost, and it is
consistently dominated by our method across the multi-objective evaluation.

\subsection{Mechanism and Applicability}\label{sec:exp-mechanism}

The previous comparison shows a systematic spill-traffic gap; we now ask where
the benefit comes from and where it applies.

\paragraph{Schedule order versus victim rule.}
With a fixed order, four Belady-style victim variants produce identical extra
traffic on Matmul and FA instances (coefficient of variation $\mathrm{CV}=0$) and
at most about $4\%$ CV on Conv rows; across different legal orders under the same
engine, extra traffic can swing by more than $10\times$.
Figure~\ref{fig:e5} decomposes an L1 high-pressure window from a benchmark case
into clean and dirty residency: under the same capacity, two legal orders expose
markedly different peak heights and dirty fractions. This confirms the prediction
of Proposition~\ref{prop:belady-margin}: when next-use distance dominates, the
victim rule has far smaller marginal effect than schedule order, which is the
main lever on spill cost.

\paragraph{Applicability domain.}
A systematic evaluation on a synthetic DAG distribution sharpens the boundary. In
the regions marked capacity-bound by the certificate of
Theorem~\ref{thm:spill-inev}, our method wins against both critical-path list
scheduling and random orders in $100\%$ of cases, and against the free-first
companion in $77.8\%$ of cases, with a median $2\times$ advantage. In the
order-reachable subgroup, a good order can avoid spills directly, leaving no room
for clean/dirty shaping, and the win rate over the free-first companion drops to
$0\%$. This matches the certificate's role: it marks the regime in which the cost
composition of unavoidable spills must be optimized, and the method's
effectiveness tracks that precondition.

\paragraph{Theory checks and surrogate behavior.}
Small-graph CP-SAT oracles verify two conclusions. For the selected order, our
engine reaches the capacity-only optimal extra traffic (ratio $1.0$). The lower
bound of Theorem~\ref{thm:spill-inev} holds on all small graphs, and the
empirical ratios for Theorem~\ref{thm:area-extra} satisfy
$E^\star/A\in[0.56,1.13]$, inside a constant-factor band. The overflow-area
surrogate $\Phi$ has almost the same global Spearman correlation with extra
traffic as per-cache peak pressure ($0.958$ versus $0.955$). The value of $\Phi$
is not a sizable accuracy gain but its $O(N)$ cost, which makes full candidate
evaluation feasible while Theorem~\ref{thm:area-extra} gives it a consistent
cross-stage justification. Per-instance figures and data are in the
supplementary material.

\paragraph{Controlled ablation.}
To rule out instance-specific artifacts, we build synthetic GEMM kernels that fix
the same DAG structure, number of spills, and peak, and change only the type of
reserve inside the peak window. Clean reserve incurs $1{,}536$ units of extra
traffic and dirty reserve incurs $3{,}072$, exactly $2\times$; under full
isolation this confirms the predicted clean/dirty cost asymmetry. A further
schedule sweep shows that extra traffic decreases monotonically as the clean
fraction at the peak rises.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{sec:exp-limitations}

The applicability boundary should be stated precisely. If an instance is
order-reachable---some legal order keeps every cache peak within capacity---then
no spill is needed and clean/dirty shaping has nothing to act on; and if a
conventional pressure/latency hybrid already preserves enough clean reserve, our
advantage shrinks or vanishes. The approximation in
Theorem~\ref{thm:area-extra} also relies on bounded absence durations, so
workloads dominated by long-lived tiny spills can weaken the surrogate. Finally,
we target a single static kernel DAG fully known at compile time; the extension
to multiple cores, dynamic control flow, and more complex memory hierarchies is
left to future work.
